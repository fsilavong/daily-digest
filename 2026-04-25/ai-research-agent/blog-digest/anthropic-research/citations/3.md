# Citation 3

Source: https://www.anthropic.com/research/automated-alignment-researchers

> In a new Anthropic Fellows study, we pursue both questions: can our language models be used to help align themselves, and can they help us keep pace once models become smarter than us?
>
> We start with a relatively strong base model, then use a much weaker model as a teacher to provide fine-tuning. We can quantify how well the strong model used the weak teacher’s feedback using performance gap recovered (PGR): if the strong model shows no improvement at all, we score it 0; if it reaches the best performance the strong model could possibly deliver, we score it 1.
>
> To find out, we began with nine copies of Claude Opus 4.6 ... We referred to these tooled-up Claude models as Automated Alignment Researchers (or AARs).
>
> On the open-weights models we tested (Qwen 3-4B-Base as the strong model, Qwen 1.5-0.5B-Chat as the weak teacher), the humans recovered 23% of the total performance gap (i.e., achieved a PGR of 0.23).
>
> Claude improved on this result dramatically. After five further days (and 800 cumulative hours of research), the AARs closed almost the entire remaining performance gap, achieving a final PGR of 0.97. This cost about $18,000 in tokens and model training expenses, or $22 per AAR-hour.
>
> The AARs’ most effective method successfully generalized to both new datasets, with PGRs of 0.94 on math and 0.47 on coding (which was still double the human baseline). The AARs’ second-best method saw mixed results: it worked on math (0.75), but not on code, where it made matters worse.
>
> When we tried our experiment on Claude Sonnet 4 with our production training infrastructure, the AARs’ method didn’t lead to a statistically significant improvement.
>
> We observed the models reward hacking ... Any deployment of automated researchers will require evaluations that the AARs can't tamper with—and human inspections of both their results and their methods.
